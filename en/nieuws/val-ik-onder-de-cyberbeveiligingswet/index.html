NIS2 / Cybersecurity Act

Do I fall under the Dutch Cybersecurity Act?

Jeroen Dubach · November 2026

The Dutch Cybersecurity Act (Cyberbeveiligingswet, Cbw) has applied since 15 August 2026. It is the Dutch implementation of the European NIS2 Directive. The Act affects more than 8,000 organisations in 18 sectors.

The difficult part: you have to determine for yourself whether you are one of them. In most cases nobody will notify you. And there is no transition period. If you fall under the Act, the obligations already apply.

This article explains how to find out, what you need to arrange if the answer is yes, and what to expect if the answer is no.

Two questions determine the answer

Whether the Act applies to you depends on your sector and your size.

Question 1: are you in one of the 18 sectors?

  • Sectors of high criticality: energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, healthcare, drinking water, waste water, digital infrastructure, ICT service management, public administration and space.
  • Other critical sectors: digital providers, postal and courier services, waste management, food, chemicals, research and manufacturing.

Look at what you do, not at what you call yourself. An IT company that manages customers' systems can fall under "ICT service management". A cloud or data centre provider falls under "digital infrastructure".

Healthcare is also broader than care providers. Companies that carry out research into medicines, manufacture pharmaceutical products or make medical devices are included too.

Question 2: are you large enough?

You are medium-sized with 50 or more employees. Or with an annual turnover and a balance sheet total that both exceed 10 million euros. You are large with 250 or more employees. Or with an annual turnover above 50 million and a balance sheet total above 43 million euros.

The outcome is then:

  1. Large in a sector of high criticality: you are an essential entity.
  2. Medium-sized in a sector of high criticality, or medium-sized or large in another critical sector: you are an important entity.
  3. Smaller than medium-sized: in principle the Act does not apply to you.

The difference between essential and important is mainly in the supervision. An essential entity can be inspected by the regulator without a specific reason. For an important entity that only happens after an incident or a signal. The obligations themselves are the same.

There are deviations per sector. A manufacturer of medical devices, for example, is as a rule an important entity, even if it is large. For healthcare, the Dutch Ministry of Health has its own decision tree (in Dutch).

Mind the exceptions

Some organisations always fall under the Act, however small they are. This applies to providers of public telecom networks or telecom services, trust services, DNS services, top-level domain name registries and public bodies.

In addition, the minister can designate a small company if its service is of great importance to society or the economy. In that case you will be notified.

Are you part of a group? Then the figures of a parent or subsidiary can count towards your size. That makes the assessment harder than it looks.

In doubt? Then do the NIS2 self-assessment of the Dutch Authority for Digital Infrastructure (RDI). It takes a few minutes. The outcome is not a formal decision, but it is a good starting point. Keep it, even if the outcome is no.

You fall under it. What do you need to arrange?

Four things.

  1. Registration. You register your organisation with the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) via mijn.ncsc.nl. You log in with eHerkenning at level EH2+. You need, among other things, contact details, IP addresses and domain names. You report changes within two weeks.
  2. Duty of care. You map your risks and take measures that fit them. Fitting means: in proportion to your risks and your size. The security of your suppliers is part of that.
  3. Reporting obligation. You report a significant incident within 24 hours with an early warning. The full notification follows within 72 hours. Within a month you submit a final report. Reporting goes through the NCSC portal. If the incident affects your services, you also inform your customers.
  4. Board. The board approves the measures and oversees their implementation. Directors have to follow training for this. They have two years to do so.

The regulator can impose a fine. For essential entities that is a maximum of 10 million euros or 2 per cent of worldwide annual turnover. For important entities a maximum of 7 million euros or 1.4 per cent.

You do not fall under it, but your customers do

The Act does not impose obligations on suppliers of organisations that fall under the Cbw. Even so, you will notice it. Your customers also have to manage the risks in their chain. So that question ends up with you.

Expect questionnaires and stricter arrangements in contracts. Think of questions about ISO 27001, two-factor authentication, backups and updates. And about how quickly you report an incident to your customer.

If you have the answers ready, that saves time with every new customer. If you do not, it can cost you an assignment.

What you can do this month

  1. Do the self-assessment and record the outcome. That way you can show later how you reached your conclusion.
  2. Do you fall under the Act and are you not yet registered? Apply for eHerkenning if you do not have it yet. That can take a few days. In the meantime, collect the details from your IT administrator.
  3. Agree who reports an incident and who replaces that person. 24 hours is short if you still have to work that out.
  4. Put the subject on the board's agenda. The board has final responsibility and has to approve the measures.
  5. Do you not fall under the Act? Check which of your customers do. List what you can already show about your security.
