Seven years after the GDPR took effect on 25 May 2018, it is time to take stock. What has the Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) done? Which sectors have been hit hardest? And what can organisations expect in 2025 and beyond?

## The figures: enforcement in perspective

In recent years the AP has imposed fines more often and at higher amounts. From the first cautious fines for municipalities and hospitals to recent decisions in the marketing and tech sectors. Total fines rose year on year, partly under pressure from the EDPB, which is pushing for effective, dissuasive sanctions.

> "Demonstrable compliance is no longer just a safeguard. It is the baseline expectation of every regulator in Europe."

## Which sectors ran the greatest risk?

- **Healthcare:** Insufficient access security for patient records and access rights that were structurally too broad
- **Public sector:** Processing without a legal basis, insufficient transparency and poor data retention
- **Marketing & retail:** Missing or invalid consent for tracking and profiling
- **HR & recruitment:** Disproportionate data collection and insufficient retention policy

## The common thread: the absence of demonstrable policy

In most cases the organisations that were fined could not show that they had taken the right measures, even when those measures actually existed. This underlines the importance of a structured privacy management system on a PDCA basis.

## What lies ahead?

The AP has announced that it will shift its enforcement priorities to AI-related data processing, large-scale tracking and the application of the Schrems II case law. Organisations that have not yet invested in a robust privacy programme run an ever greater risk.
